\begin{abstract}
A mineral prospectivity map stacks geophysical, geochemical and structural evidence over a study area and outputs,
per cell, the probability that a deposit is there. Such maps are typically presented with an area-under-curve
(AUC) and a posterior, and both numbers can be inflated in two specific, well-understood ways.
ProspectMap is an interactive tool that computes such a map by Weights of Evidence, a Bayesian log-odds update,
and makes those two inflations \emph{first-class}, measuring them rather than hiding them. This report quantifies
both on the real US Midcontinent Mississippi-Valley-type zinc-lead belt (the open Critical Minerals Mapping
Initiative dataset of Lawley \emph{et al.}, 2022) and on ten synthetic cases. The first inflation is
\emph{evaluation}: reporting AUC under random cross-validation, which lets spatially adjacent cells leak between
train and test, overstates prospective skill. On the real belt, with thresholds, weights and prior fitted on the
training folds only and every map cell scored, the Weights-of-Evidence AUC falls from $0.74$ under random
cross-validation to $0.65$ under spatial block cross-validation, an inflation of $0.09$, the second largest of the
eleven cases. Under the same spatial folds a learned classifier reaches $0.91$, but a baseline that ranks cells by
their distance to the nearest training deposit reaches $0.90$, so this protocol does not show that the classifier
learned more than proximity to known deposits. The second inflation is \emph{modelling}: Weights of Evidence
assumes the evidence layers are conditionally independent given a deposit, and on the real belt they are not. The
posterior probabilities sum to $1124$ against $858$ deposit cells (omnibus $z = 8.3$), so the posterior is
overconfident. The six layers' contrasts are individually significant (studentized contrasts $2.45$ to $5.80$;
exact one-sided $p \le 1.9\times10^{-3}$), but four of them come from thresholds that leave no deposit outside the
pattern, where the weight outside the pattern is set by a zero-count correction. The tool displays the spatial-CV
AUC, the conditional-independence test and the per-layer significance beside the inflated values; every number
and figure can be regenerated from the released artifacts.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{W}{here} is the next ore deposit most likely to be? Mineral prospectivity mapping answers it by
overlaying evidence layers, a magnetic high here, a geochemical anomaly there, proximity to a favourable
structure, over a study-area grid and computing, per cell, a posterior probability of a deposit. The classical
method is Weights of Evidence~\cite{bonham94,bonham89}: each binary evidence pattern gets a positive weight
$W^+$ where it is present and $W^-$ where it is absent, their difference is the contrast $C=W^+-W^-$, and the
posterior log-odds of a deposit in a cell is the prior log-odds plus the sum of the present-or-absent weights.
The output is a map, and the map is usually reported with two numbers: a high area-under-curve showing it separates
known deposits from barren ground, and confident-looking weights. Both are, in a well-documented way, prone to
inflation.

ProspectMap is a tool built to expose the inflation. It computes the Weights-of-Evidence
posterior live, and it makes first-class the two ways the numbers lie. The first is that an AUC reported under
\emph{random} cross-validation is optimistic, because a prospectivity grid is spatially autocorrelated and a
random split leaves near-neighbour cells in both train and test, so the model is partly tested on what it has
seen; \emph{spatial} cross-validation, holding out contiguous blocks, gives a realistic estimate~\cite{roberts}. The
second is that Weights of Evidence assumes the evidence layers are conditionally independent given a deposit, and
when they are not the posterior is overconfident~\cite{agterberg}; and a contrast can look large because of
how its binarization threshold was chosen. This report quantifies both, on the real US Mississippi-Valley-type
zinc-lead belt from the open Critical Minerals Mapping Initiative dataset~\cite{lawley} and on ten synthetic
cases (four terranes, two data regimes and four controls), and reports a learned classifier~\cite{rodriguez} and
a distance-to-deposit baseline under the same folds. The contribution is the measurement and the display of the two
inflations, not a new prospectivity algorithm.

\section{The First Lie: Cross-Validation Inflation}\label{sec:cv}

The first inflation is a matter of how the map is scored, and it is quantified in Fig.~\ref{fig:inflation}a. For
every case, the Weights-of-Evidence posterior is evaluated two ways: random five-fold cross-validation, which
splits the grid cells at random, and spatial five-fold cross-validation, which holds out contiguous
$20\times20$-cell blocks so that the test set is geographically separate from the training set. In both, each
layer's binarization threshold, its weights and the prior are fitted on the training folds' cells only, the
held-out fold is only scored, and every map cell receives one held-out score. Random cross-validation is at least
as optimistic in every case except K-OROGENIC and D-SPARSE, where the spatial AUC is higher by $0.001$ and
$0.053$, because a prospectivity grid is strongly spatially autocorrelated and a random split leaves a test
cell's near neighbours in the training set, so the model is scored partly on cells like ones it has seen. On the
\emph{real} US Mississippi-Valley-type belt the gap is the second largest of the eleven cases, after the
synthetic K-IOCG terrane ($0.110$): the Weights-of-Evidence AUC falls from $0.739$ under random cross-validation
to $0.648$ under spatial, an inflation of $0.091$. The spatial estimate is the relevant one; the random one
overstates how well the map would find a deposit in unseen ground.

Fig.~\ref{fig:inflation}b sets these values beside the Weights-of-Evidence AUC without cross-validation ($0.732$,
weights fitted and scored on the whole map) and beside two other models scored on every map cell with the same
folds. A learned classifier, a small multilayer perceptron on the six continuous evidence layers (Weights of
Evidence uses their binarized patterns), reaches $0.939$ under random and $0.908$ under spatial
cross-validation. A baseline that learns no geology and ranks each cell by its distance to the nearest training
deposit reaches $0.960$ and $0.899$: the classifier exceeds it by $0.010$ under spatial folds and falls below it
under random ones. Spatial blocks of $20\times20$ cells still leave each held-out block beside training blocks,
so this protocol cannot separate what the classifier learned from the evidence layers from proximity to known
deposits. A logistic regression on the same binary patterns, also fitted out of fold, tracks Weights of Evidence
($0.744$ random, $0.644$ spatial). On its own labelled sample, the 858 deposit cells and 2574 sampled non-deposit
cells more than two cells from any deposit, scored as the mean of per-fold AUCs, the classifier gives $0.982$ and
$0.946$; those values come from a different cell set and are not comparable with the all-cells ones.

\section{The Second Lie: An Overconfident Posterior}\label{sec:woe}

\begin{figure*}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.66\textwidth]{fig-woe.pdf}
\caption{The weights of the real US-MVT belt. Weights-of-Evidence contrasts $C=W^+-W^-$ (bars) and studentized
contrasts $C/s(C)$ (line) of the six evidence layers at the thresholds chosen on all deposits, with the one-sided
($1.645$) and two-sided ($1.96$) $5\%$ levels. Every layer exceeds both. The four starred layers have no deposit
cell outside their pattern, so their $W^-$, $C$ and $s(C)$ rest on the $0.5$ zero-count correction.}
\label{fig:woe}
\end{figure*}

The second inflation is in the model. Weights of Evidence assumes the evidence layers are conditionally
independent given a deposit; when correlated layers are stacked, their weights double-count the same signal and
the posterior becomes overconfident, which is why a conditional-independence test~\cite{agterberg} belongs in any
Weights-of-Evidence workflow. On the real belt the assumption fails. Under conditional independence the posterior
probabilities summed over the map equal, in expectation, the number of deposit cells; here they sum to
$T = 1124$ against $N(D) = 858$, with $s(T) = 32$, so the omnibus test gives $z = 8.3$~\cite{agterberg} and the
conditional-independence ratio $N(D)/T$ is $0.76$. The posterior probabilities therefore overstate the number of
deposits by $31\%$. The pairwise tests, stratified by deposit presence, locate the dependence: the \texttt{lab}
and \texttt{faultprox} patterns are the most dependent pair (Cram\'er's $V = 0.79$), followed by \texttt{grav}
with \texttt{faultprox} ($0.31$) and \texttt{grav} with \texttt{lab} ($0.28$).

The individual weights are a separate question (Fig.~\ref{fig:woe}). All six contrasts are statistically
significant: their studentized contrasts $C/s(C)$ range from $2.45$ (\texttt{mag}) to $5.80$
(\texttt{marginprox}), and exact one-sided Fisher tests on the six $2\times2$ tables give $p \le 1.9\times10^{-3}$
for every layer. Their sizes, however, reflect how the thresholds were chosen. Each layer is binarized at the
threshold that maximizes $C$ among $40$ candidates with $C/s(C) \ge 1.5$, and for \texttt{grav}, \texttt{lab},
\texttt{satgrav} and
\texttt{faultprox} that threshold puts every deposit cell inside the pattern. $W^-$ then rests on a zero count and
on the correction of $0.5$ that replaces it; with a correction of $0.1$ the same four layers give $C/s(C)$ between
$1.81$ and $2.48$. The exact tests do not depend on the correction, but the magnitudes of $C$, up to $6.2$, do, and
they are maxima over $40$ thresholds on the same data, with $s(C)$ computed as if the $25{,}344$ cells were
independent, although the layers and the deposits are spatially autocorrelated. The weights are significant, but
not as decisive as their raw contrasts suggest.

\section{Discussion and Scope}\label{sec:discuss}

The two inflations are independent and both matter. A map can pass the conditional-independence test and still
report an inflated AUC because it was scored with random cross-validation; or it can report a spatially
cross-validated AUC and still carry an overconfident posterior. ProspectMap's contribution is to compute the
Weights-of-Evidence map and then put the deflating diagnostics next to it: the spatial-CV AUC beside the random
one, the conditional-independence test beside the posterior, and the studentized contrast, flagged when it rests
on a zero-count correction, beside the raw one. A learned classifier and a distance-to-deposit baseline are
reported under the same folds; on the real belt the classifier's spatial-CV AUC ($0.908$) is far above that of
Weights of Evidence ($0.648$), but the distance baseline reaches $0.899$, so the comparison ranks proximity to
known deposits as much as geology. The ten synthetic cases, four terranes, two data regimes and four controls,
exercise the diagnostics under known ground truth; the one real case is the open Critical Minerals Mapping
Initiative US-MVT dataset~\cite{lawley}, on which spatial cross-validation removes the second-largest AUC
inflation of the eleven cases and the conditional-independence test fails with $z = 8.3$. The tool is a
decision-support and teaching instrument, not an exploration recommendation for any specific ground; it says where
the evidence points and, more importantly, how much to trust it. The interface shows the same diagnostics on
every map.

\section{Conclusion}

A mineral prospectivity map lies in two well-understood ways: it reports an AUC inflated by random
cross-validation, and its Weights-of-Evidence posterior is overconfident when the evidence layers are not
conditionally independent given a deposit. ProspectMap measures both. On the real US Mississippi-Valley-type belt
the Weights-of-Evidence AUC falls from $0.74$ to $0.65$ when scored by spatial instead of random
cross-validation, with thresholds, weights and prior fitted on the training folds only; a learned classifier
reaches $0.91$ under the same spatial folds, where a baseline that ranks cells by their distance to the nearest
training deposit reaches $0.90$; and the posterior probabilities sum to $1.31$ times the number of deposit cells
(conditional-independence $z = 8.3$). The six layers' contrasts are individually significant, although four rest
on thresholds that leave no deposit outside the pattern. The contribution is a reproducible tool that displays
these diagnostics next to the inflated values.
